12 Study Tips Backed by Research: Remember, Keep Going, Plan

Studied a lot but can't remember it? Can't keep it going? Small changes in how you study can help. Here are twelve tips, limited to what learning research has actually found. You won't see "guaranteed" or "X% better" here, because the research supports "tends to", not promises.

Remember

Recall instead of rereading

Trying to recall something without looking tends to stick longer than rereading it. Close your notes and write down what you remember, solve practice problems, or quiz yourself with flashcards. Whatever you couldn't recall is where to start next time.

In Study Buddy: Leave a one-line note of what you couldn't recall after you stop. It gives your next session a starting point.

Reference: research on the testing effect (Roediger & Karpicke 2006); Dunlosky et al. 2013

Spread it out over several days

For the same amount of time, spreading your study over several days helps you remember longer than cramming it into one night. It also helps to quickly review what you did the day before, then start.

In Study Buddy: In "All records", you can see when you last studied the same subject.

Reference: research on distributed practice (Cepeda et al. 2006); Dunlosky et al. 2013

Mix up the problem types

In one study, students who practiced a mix of problem types did better on the test than those who practiced one type at a time. Mixing feels harder, because you are also practicing how to choose the right method.

In Study Buddy: Recording by subject shows how much you practiced each area.

Reference: research on interleaved practice (Rohrer & Taylor 2007)

Explain it to yourself

Reading while explaining "why" to yourself, out loud or in your head, tends to deepen understanding. The parts you can't explain are the parts you don't understand yet.

In Study Buddy: Try writing one line in your own words about what you learned today in the note after you stop.

Reference: research on self-explanation (Chi et al. 1994)

Don't stop at highlighting and rereading

Highlighting and rereading make it feel like you remember, but on their own they don't stick well. If you use them, finish with a few minutes of recalling without looking.

In Study Buddy: Make the last five minutes of your session "recall time", and keep the timer running through it.

Reference: Dunlosky et al. 2013

Keep going

Decide when, where, and what

"I'll study when I have time" is easier to skip than "After dinner, at my desk, vocabulary for 20 minutes." Deciding in advance makes it easier to follow through.

In Study Buddy: Add when and what to your study plan, and it shows on the home screen.

Reference: research on implementation intentions (Gollwitzer 1999)

Same time, same place

Doing the same thing in the same situation every day gradually makes it easier to start without thinking. How long this takes varies a lot from person to person.

In Study Buddy: Your streak and the study calendar show how it adds up.

Reference: research on habit formation (Lally et al. 2010)

It's okay to miss a day

In research on building habits, missing one day did not have much effect on how the habit formed. What matters is starting again.

In Study Buddy: Your buddy never shrinks when you take a break.

Reference: Lally et al. 2010

Don't cut your sleep

Sleep plays a role in helping what you learned settle into memory. Especially before a test, try not to trade sleep for cramming.

In Study Buddy: In "All records", you can look back at what times you studied. If late nights keep showing up, try starting a little earlier.

Reference: review on sleep and memory (Diekelmann & Born 2010)

Plan

Make your goal specific

Specific goals, like "solve three past exam papers", tend to lead to better results than "do my best". Goals that are too high can make it harder to keep going.

In Study Buddy: Try writing a specific goal in "Current goal".

Reference: research on goal setting (Locke & Latham 2002)

Allow more time than you think

People tend to underestimate how long tasks will take (the planning fallacy). Plans with some extra room are less likely to fall apart.

In Study Buddy: Your records show how long things actually took, which helps you estimate your next plan.

Reference: research on the planning fallacy (Buehler et al. 1994)

Break big tasks into small ones

Listing small steps, like "workbook p.40–45" and "redo 3 problems", instead of "do math", brings your time estimates closer to reality. Finishing each one also shows you are making progress.

In Study Buddy: Your study plan imports one line at a time, so you can paste small steps.

Reference: Kruger & Evans 2004

About Study Buddy

Study Buddy is a study timer where a small buddy grows as you log study time. It supports Pomodoro, records by subject, study plans, exam countdowns and a Lock Screen timer. If you miss a day, your buddy never shrinks and never scolds you.
